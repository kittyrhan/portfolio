01Project framing & heuristic review

The app said two different things

This project examines how riders experience and navigate uncertainty during the waiting phase of Uber rides, using real-world usability testing to identify breakdowns and opportunities for clearer, more trustworthy interactions. The project is sponsored by Uber, collaborating with UW HCDE's 517: Usability Testing Course.

The waiting screen used “Congratulations!” before a driver had been found. It also said “Confirming your ride” while the system was still searching. Together with “looking for a ride,” the language blurred the distinction between searching, matching, and confirming.

At pickup, control was limited when something felt wrong: a driver on the wrong side of the street, an unclear pickup point, or an “arrived” status while the vehicle faced the wrong direction. Riders needed better ways to intervene and communicate before canceling or abandoning the ride.

02Usability testing

Follow riders through a real trip

We tested with seven college students, all iPhone users, with varied riding frequency and familiarity with Wait & Save. They requested rides from Westlake Link Station to Chinatown Link Station in a high-demand urban environment, between 11 AM and 3 PM.

The study followed three moments. First, riders confirmed the request and explained what they had committed to and expected next. Then they sent a researcher a message with their arrival time. Finally, they monitored the approaching driver and explained where the driver was and what they should do while waiting.

03Research findings

Four breakdowns emerged across requesting, matching, and pickup.

Cancellation visibility

A familiar action moved when the state changed

Before driver assignment, 6 out of 6 participants quickly opened the draggable sheet [A] to find the cancel button [B].

“So my first impression is that it’s not very visible at first glance. But I know that if I pull [the tab] up, it’s gonna have like the little button right there for you to cancel.”

Participant 1

After assignment, 2 out of 6 participants tried other screens or buttons before finding cancellation. Four out of 7 first scrolled to the bottom—as they had before matching—then went back to the top to find it in Ride details.

Arrival notifications

Arrival updates did not reach everyone

Expectation dropped when the system changed without surfacing the change. Four out of 7 participants did not receive “Your driver is arriving.” Of those four, two did not receive it inside the app and one did not receive it outside the app. P3 received “Check your ride” without an arrival alert; P6’s iPhone settings did not reflect how they wanted to be notified during a ride.

Driver-matching status

Show what happens inside the time range

Riders understood the estimated waiting range, but not what the system was doing within it.

Waiting tolerance

The wait mattered more than the savings

Riders evaluate the value of Wait & Save primarily based on whether the waiting time feels acceptable in their situation. Price savings play a secondary role. 6 out of 7 riders referenced waiting tolerance, while 2 out of 7 referenced price savings.

Before confirming, estimated waiting ranges help riders judge whether the wait feels acceptable. Once riders enter the driver-matching waiting room, the interface provides limited cues to confirm whether the wait is still within their tolerance and whether the cost-time trade-off remains worthwhile. This may lead to impatience and cancellations.

04Design recommendations

Translate the observed breakdowns into clearer controls and system feedback.

Cancellation visibility

Our recommendation was to make cancellation immediately visible and keep its location consistent before and after driver assignment.

Arrival notifications

We recommended giving riders a choice in how a time-sensitive ride reaches them, so ride alerts can match their needs in that moment.

Driver-matching status

The opportunity was to make driver matching legible without adding noise.

Waiting tolerance

We recommended restoring reassurance throughout the waiting process, so riders could reassess whether the wait still suited their situation.

These are recommendations from the usability study. A follow-up study would be needed to evaluate their effect on the waiting experience.

05Reflection & next steps

Give the study room to breathe

Next time, we would save the post-task questionnaire until after the ride and give participants a break from high-pressure tasks and conversation. More prepared probe questions would help us gather richer, more consistent insights across variable waits. Keeping the same research roles and using more than one notetaker would also improve data consistency.
